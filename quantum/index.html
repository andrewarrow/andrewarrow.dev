What the quantum consciousness experiments measured

In 2023, researchers shone ultraviolet light on microtubules, the protein tubes that help cells hold their shape. From the fluorescence, they estimated that an excitation could travel about 6.6 nanometers. Adding an anesthetic shortened that distance.

It’s an intriguing connection: a drug that alters consciousness also alters energy transfer in a structure found inside neurons. But the sample was purified protein in solution. What carried the excitation, and would the same process matter inside a neuron?

Reading these papers as one accumulating case for quantum consciousness misses their disagreements. They propose different mechanisms, and some of the experiments don’t involve consciousness at all.

Explain it like I’m 5

Your brain is full of cells passing messages. Together they let you see, think, and feel. We can measure a lot of that activity, but we still don’t have an agreed explanation for how it becomes the feeling of being you.

Inside those cells are tiny protein tubes. At a much smaller scale, the stuff they’re made of follows quantum rules: things can behave like waves, with several possibilities contributing to what happens. Some scientists think those effects could help the brain make a thought. The hard part is showing how they survive all the jostling inside a brain and affect what its cells do.

So far, scientists have measured how isolated proteins absorb and give off light, and how animals respond to an anesthetic. We don’t yet know whether those results explain why you have an inner experience.

Energy transfer in microtubules

In Kalra and colleagues’ 2023 experiment, ultraviolet light excited purified proteins in solution at room temperature. A fluorescent quencher attached to the proteins shortened the light-emission lifetime. From that change, the team inferred how far the excitation could diffuse through the structure. This gives an estimated two-dimensional diffusion length, not a direct observation of an excitation traveling along a tube.

The conventional Förster-based simulations gave 1.11 or 1.54 nanometers under two different assumptions about which amino acids exchange energy. The estimates differ by several times from the experiment, so the transfer model deserves another look. The paper discusses short-range orbital overlap, which a point-dipole approximation misses. That offers an explanation to investigate before attributing the discrepancy to long-lived quantum coherence. The authors themselves describe their diffusion length as an order-of-magnitude estimate.

The anesthetic result is what makes this tempting to connect to consciousness. But these drugs act on many targets. To make that connection, the same energy-transfer process would have to matter in a working neuron, and changing it would have to explain some part of the drug’s effect on experience.

Fluorescence yield and superradiance

Babcock and colleagues’ 2024 paper models superradiance, in which emitters act collectively and radiate faster. Its experiments measured fluorescence quantum yield: the share of absorbed photons that emerge as emitted light. At 295-nanometer excitation, the reported yield was 10.9 ± 1.3% for tubulin dimers and 14.7 ± 1.6% for microtubules.

A higher yield can result from faster light emission or less energy lost through other routes. The authors explicitly say that complementary lifetime measurements are needed to determine the radiative rate; they did not perform them. The much larger networks in their calculations were modeled structures; the experiments used isolated protein preparations.

Xenon isotopes in mice

Xenon provides a more direct connection to nervous-system behavior. Its isotopes have nearly identical chemistry but different nuclear spins. In Li and colleagues’ 2018 study, 80 male mice were split into four groups of 20. Each group received a different xenon isotope alongside 0.50% isoflurane.

The endpoint was loss of the righting reflex: a mouse placed on its back failed to roll over within ten seconds. The spin-bearing isotopes required more xenon to reach that endpoint. Higher concentration means lower anesthetic potency in this experiment.

The 0.50% isoflurane matters here. The paper’s xenon-alone concentrations were calculated by assuming the two anesthetics’ effects add together; the table above shows what was measured with both drugs present. A righting-reflex result tells us about anesthetic behavior; it doesn’t identify the molecular process or tell us what an animal experienced.

A radical-pair explanation

Smith and colleagues’ radical-pair paper, published in 2021, tries to explain those same mouse results. A radical pair consists of two molecules with unpaired electrons. In the proposed mechanism, interactions involving xenon’s nuclear spin change the pair’s spin evolution and chemical reaction yields. They place the hypothetical pair near an NMDA receptor, a protein involved in neural signaling.

The calculation can fit the isotope potency ratios by adjusting coupling, reaction, and coherence parameters. But the proposed radical pair has not been identified in that biological setting. One fitted parameter set assumes a microsecond of spin coherence. The authors acknowledge that free superoxide, one candidate partner, is expected to lose that coherence in under a nanosecond. Binding could change its behavior, but the required protection remains to be demonstrated.

The model also predicts field-dependent changes in isotope potency, which could be tested against measurements it was not fitted to. As it stands, the calculation and the mouse experiment share one set of animal data.

From quantum chemistry to consciousness

Quantum mechanics already applies to every molecule in your brain. The interesting question is whether a particular quantum process does something essential for thought or conscious experience. Calling the brain “quantum” doesn’t identify that process.

A superposition combines possible states in a way that can produce interference, rather like waves reinforcing or canceling one another. Those relationships are delicate. As a system interacts with its surroundings, the interference can become inaccessible: decoherence. A warm, wet brain gives a proposed mechanism plenty of interactions to survive.

Temperature alone doesn’t settle the question. The proposed state, its lifetime, and its effect on the surrounding biology have to be specified. The microtubule experiments concern excitation transfer over nanometers in prepared samples. Preserving a state across neurons would be a further result, and explaining conscious experience would require more again.

What Orch-OR adds

Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff developed orchestrated objective reduction, or Orch-OR, in the 1990s, with a central account published in 1996. Their 2014 review sets out the theory and replies to objections.

Hameroff supplies the candidate biological machinery: microtubules, hollow tubes assembled from a protein called tubulin. They help organize cells and give neurons structure. He proposes that they can also support protected quantum states coordinated within and between neurons. Penrose supplies an additional physical claim: certain superpositions become unstable because of gravity and undergo an objective collapse. Orch-OR identifies these orchestrated collapses with moments of conscious experience.

That last step goes beyond ordinary quantum chemistry. Penrose argues that consciousness requires something classical computation cannot supply; the proposed collapse is meant to supply it. Finding unusual optical behavior in tubulin would still leave the coordinated states, the collapse mechanism, and the link to experience to establish.

A collapse model meets an underground detector

There is a way to test some collapse physics without going near a brain. In a particular mathematical model of gravity-related collapse, the same noise that localizes matter also makes charged particles emit radiation. Donadi and colleagues used a germanium detector at Gran Sasso to look for that radiation, with the mountain helping shield it from cosmic-ray background.

Their result ruled out the natural, parameter-free version of the Diósi–Penrose model they tested. The bound requires its mass-smearing length, a parameter describing how spread out the model treats nuclear mass, to exceed 5.4 × 10−11 meters at 95% probability. The exclusion depends on the specified mass distribution and collapse dynamics. This constrains that formulation of collapse, rather than directly testing microtubules or consciousness.

A different proposal from Hartmut Neven

Hartmut Neven and his coauthors take a different position in their 2024 proposal. They associate a conscious moment with the formation of a superposition and dispense with gravity-driven collapse. Their account uses the many-worlds interpretation, with its parallel branches of reality. The proposed connection between those branches and experience is an additional conjecture.

They ask whether coherently coupling a brain to a quantum processor could enlarge the joint superposition and produce a richer reported experience. Ordinary electrical recording or stimulation would not establish the required quantum link. Before that experiment, the authors propose testing whether a biological sample can mediate entanglement between two controlled qubits, while excluding a direct connection between them. Even a successful result would establish a quantum channel, leaving its role in experience to test.

Neven’s 2026 conference abstract reports behavioral isotope effects in fruit flies exposed to xenon and nitrous oxide, alongside NMR and electrophysiological studies of human brain organoids. Organoids are simplified models of neural tissue. The abstract gives no sample sizes, effect estimates, or full methods. Those are needed to assess the reported effects; a functioning brain–quantum computer interface is still a proposal.

The next useful result would connect the measurements across scales: identify a quantum process in relevant tissue, show that it survives there, and change it selectively while measuring neural activity. A claim about conscious experience would then need an experiment that measures experience, with controls for ordinary effects of the intervention.

The strongest reason to keep working on these ideas is that some of the disagreements are testable. Change the magnetic field during a xenon isotope experiment. Measure fluorescence lifetimes alongside quantum yields in microtubules. Those results could constrain specific mechanisms. Calling every positive result evidence for “quantum consciousness” loses the detail that makes the experiments useful.
